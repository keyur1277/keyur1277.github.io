Roots of Meanings

Abstract

Ferdinand de Saussure is among the most influential linguists of the modern era. He proposed that there is no natural, inherent, or logical connection between a word’s form (the signifier) and its meaning (the signified); the pairing is fixed by arbitrary social convention. Saussure did not claim that every linguistic unit is assigned in this way — once a core inventory of arbitrarily assigned signifiers is in place, further derivation from that core can occur non-arbitrarily. This principle — arbitrariness at the root, convention-bound derivation above it — is a foundation stone of most modern linguistic theory.

Pāṇini (पाणिनि), writing in a more distant past, described a logical system by which all Saṃskṛta (संस्कृत) words are derived from smaller constituents, with word meaning arising compositionally from those constituents. The constituents themselves — upasarga (उपसर्ग), dhātu (धातु), and pratyaya (प्रत्यय) — are, in the received tradition, assigned meaning by social/hereditary convention. In effect, Pāṇini narrows the universe of arbitrarily assigned signifiers, but does not eliminate arbitrariness — it is simply relocated to a smaller, more fundamental set.

This paper presents a hypothesis that the varṇa/akṣara (वर्ण/अक्षर, the individual phonemes of Saṃskṛta) carry intrinsic meaning, and that the constituents Pāṇini treats as his atomic building blocks have derived, not assigned, meanings. The author’s working position is that varṇa meanings themselves are not socially assigned either — that they are rooted in the evolution of the brain — but establishing that deeper claim lies beyond the scope of this paper and is left open for future research.

This hypothesis proposes that words (signifiers) have an evolutionary genealogy, rather than the instantaneous, “Genesis-like” genealogy implied by Saussurean arbitrariness.

Hypothesis and Its Testing

In Saṃskṛta, all words (pada, पद) are derived words. The building blocks from which final pada are constructed are upasarga, dhātu, and pratyaya. Pāṇini describes1 a set of processes applied to these building blocks depending on the concept being expressed, and also gives an exhaustive list of exceptions to those processes. Saṃskṛta possesses a very strong tradition of deriving word meaning from word constituents — but that tradition of derivation stops at the list of upasarga, dhātu, and pratyaya themselves; their own meanings are treated as given, not derived.

The hypothesis this paper presents is that Saṃskṛta varṇa carry intrinsic meaning, and that upasarga, dhātu, and pratyaya do not have arbitrarily assigned meaning or function — their meanings are instead derived from the meanings of their constituent varṇa.

These varṇa meanings have been lost over time and must be deciphered. If all words are derived words, then the meanings of varṇa can, in principle, be recovered by working backward from the words built from them.

If the hypothesis is correct, the meanings of upasarga, dhātu, and — especially — pratyaya should each yield a coherent, meaningful interpretation when derived from their varṇa constituents. Given that there are approximately 2,000 dhātu, 22 upasarga, and 2,000–4,000 pratyaya, a random, coincidental match across a large portion of this corpus — while simultaneously keeping the polysemy of each varṇa compact and semantically coherent — is statistically implausible. A formal version of this argument is developed in the Findings section below.

Decipher Process

Curiosity Phase

Sometime between 2022 and 2023, while listening to the word vasudevasutaṃdeva (वसुदेवसुतंदेव), the author became curious that varṇa might carry their own independent meanings, and that words might derive their meaning from these constituent meanings. Out of that curiosity, an initial decipherment of varṇa meanings was undertaken — before the author had encountered Pāṇini’s work. This early decipherment drew on Gujarati, Hindi, and Saṃskṛta.

All thirteen svara (स्वर, vowels) were excluded from this phase of decipherment, since a vowel can combine with any consonant. A grid of the thirty-three consonants (vyañjana, व्यंजन) was constructed, yielding 1,089 possible two-consonant varṇa combinations. For each pair, real words containing that consonant pair (plus any accompanying svara) were sought in dictionaries, and a candidate varṇa meaning was inferred from the resulting word list. At this stage, the function of svara had not yet been identified. Part of this decipherment was also inspired by structural similarities between the Brāhmī (ब्राह्मी) and Proto-Sinaitic scripts.

Decipher Phase

The study of varṇa subsequently led to the works of Pāṇini. The meanings already deciphered were tested against the list and meanings of dhātu, with fitment attempted against an agricultural, or at earliest a hunter-gatherer, context. The meanings of vyañjana were refined to align with the known meanings of dhātu.

Sometime between July and August 2024, ṛ (ऋ) and ḷ (ऌ) were deciphered by treating them as aspṛśya (अस्पृश्य) and as svara. This led to the decipherment of the entire svara portion, and to the realization that svara were the historical predecessors of the function later assumed by vibhakti (विभक्ति, grammatical case) in Pāṇini’s description.

With svara reinterpreted as proto-vibhakti markers, dhātu were re-checked for fitment against the existing varṇa decipherment. It became apparent that multiple layers of historical context were present, and that the most ancient layer decisively predated the age of agriculture by a substantial margin. Varṇa meanings were accordingly refined to a pre-agricultural context.

M (म), n (न), and ṇ (ण) were deciphered early in this process; around the same period, ṅ (ङ) was deciphered based on aṅga (अङ्ग) and aṅgula (अङ्गुल), with support from English cognates. Ñ (ञ) was understood from its established sense of śukra (शुक्र).

By late 2025, the deciphered varṇa meanings were committed to a version-controlled repository (git) as a time-stamped reference record.

The most recently updated deciphered varṇa meanings are given in Annexure 1. For the historical evolution of the decipherment, the git commit history should be consulted.

Following are guidelines for interpreting these meanings.

Framework for Interpretation

The following framework is the reference point for interpretation.

Step 1: The Pāṇinian Layer

  1. Every Saṃskṛta pada can be derived in two ways: (1) as a pada derived through the Pāṇinian system of derivation — referred to here as pada derivation — or (2) as a varṇa sequence, or “soup” — referred to here as varṇa-samāsa (वर्ण समास). Varṇa-samāsa is discussed in Step 2.
  2. Pada derivation proceeds through upasarga, dhātu, pratyaya, and the Pāṇinian processes that combine them into a final pada. The meaning of the resulting pada is derived from its constituents; the derivation process itself is treated as semantically empty. This leaves the meanings of upasarga, dhātu, and pratyaya themselves still to be accounted for.
  3. The meanings of upasarga, dhātu, and pratyaya can be derived only through the varṇa-samāsa process — that is, by reading them as varṇa strings.

Step 2: The Varṇa-Samāsa Layer

In this step, varṇa-samāsa meanings are derived from varṇa meanings, according to the following guidelines:

  1. Every dhātu (in its mūla/मूल form), every regular pratyaya, every upasarga, every āgama (आगम), every ādeśa (आदेश), every lakāra (लकार), every other pratyaya including uṇādi (उणादि), and every nipāta (निपात) is to be interpreted as varṇa-samāsa.
  2. Distinctions of tiṅ-sup (तिङ्-सुप्), kāla (काल, tense), vacana (वचन, number), puruṣa (पुरुष, person), and liṅga (लिङ्ग, gender) are to be disregarded; varṇa meaning is treated as fungible across all of these distinctions.
  3. At the varṇa level, the noun/verb distinction is lost; a varṇa represents both simultaneously.
  4. A svara may be interpreted either as a varṇa in its own right or as a svara-vibhakti (स्वर विभक्ति). In the latter role, a svara functions as a case marker on both the vyañjana preceding it and the one following it.
  5. Ā (आ) and a (अ) are treated as interchangeable. An a (अ) appended to a hal (हल्)/vyañjana is disregarded.
  6. Dīrgha (दीर्घ, long), hrasva (ह्रस्व, short), and pluta (प्लुत, protracted) vowel lengths are treated as interchangeable.
  7. Udātta (उदात्त), anudātta (अनुदात्त), and svarita (स्वरित) accents are treated as interchangeable.
  8. The full context associated with a varṇa’s meaning is understood to be invoked whenever that varṇa is referenced.
  9. The meaning of a varṇa-samāsa is to be understood with the aid of context. The relevant context is defined as one in which all constituent varṇa meanings would plausibly have been held simultaneously in the working memory of some Homo or great ape, at some point between 7 Ma and 1 Ma.
  10. Anusvāra (अनुस्वार) may be interpreted as any nasal.

Findings

Why a 70% or 80% match rate at this sample size could not arise by chance

The hypothesis under test makes a falsifiable statistical claim. It does not require that every dhātu, upasarga, and pratyaya examined so far has been successfully derived from varṇa meanings: full decipherment of the ~4,000–6,000-item target corpus (≈2,000 dhātu, 22 upasarga, 2,000–4,000 pratyaya) remains in progress (see Future Plan, below). What the framework does claim is that, within the portion of the corpus examined so far and reported in Annexures 2–4 (21 upasarga, 331 dhātu, 99 pratyaya — 451 items in total, the pratyaya count combining the 56 folder-tagged entries with 43 further pratyaya explained in the author’s commentary: काम्यच्, क्त्रि, मप्, क्नु, the 18-cell तिङ् table, and the 21-cell सुप् table), a coherent varṇa-samāsa derivation succeeds at a rate far above anything a random, arbitrary pairing of varṇa and meaning could plausibly produce at this sample size. Annexure 5 additionally reports 450 general पद (words) from the same corpus; these are not part of this statistical claim, since the hypothesis as framed in this paper concerns the three grammatical building blocks (उपसर्ग, धातु, प्रत्यय) specifically, and पद meaning is treated as a downstream consequence of those three rather than as independent evidence.

Match rate: pending independent human review. The success/failure judgment for each item in Annexures 2–4 is to be made by an independent human checker performing a manual, item-by-item review — comparing each entry’s componential (वर्ण-समास) reading against its independently attested rūḍha artha or grammatical function — rather than by the author or by an automated process. That review is not yet complete; the calculation below is therefore presented parametrically, for the two benchmark match rates under discussion (70% and 80%), so that the statistical conclusion is available as soon as the checker reports an actual figure, without needing to redo the mathematics.

Binomial calculation. Let n be the sample size, p₀ the assumed chance-success rate under the null (Saussurean, arbitrary-assignment) hypothesis, and k the number of items an independent checker scores as a match. The probability of observing at least k successes in n independent trials under the null hypothesis is:

P(X ≥ k | n, p₀) = Σ (from i=k to n) [ C(n, i) × p₀ⁱ × (1−p₀)ⁿ⁻ⁱ ]

Interpretation. For the dhātu and pratyaya categories, and for the combined corpus, a 70% or 80% match rate is astronomically improbable under the null hypothesis at every chance baseline considered, including the deliberately generous 50% coin-flip baseline. The upasarga category is the honest exception: at n = 21, a 70% match rate is only marginally improbable under a 50% chance baseline (p ≈ 0.04) — a real result, but not an overwhelming one on its own. This is a direct, expected consequence of sample size, not a flaw in the argument: small samples have less statistical power, and the upasarga result should be read as suggestive on its own while drawing its real force from being consistent with the much stronger dhātu and pratyaya results, and from the combined-corpus row.

Caveats. (1) No match/success figure is asserted here as an actual finding — the table is a ready-reference for whatever rate the independent human checker reports, at both benchmark levels under discussion. (2) The chance baseline p₀ cannot be fixed with certainty; the table’s inclusion of p₀ = 0.5 is a deliberate concession to the null hypothesis, not a claim that 50% is the true or likely chance rate. (3) This calculation establishes non-randomness — that a 70–80% match rate is not a pattern arbitrary assignment would plausibly produce — not orderliness in the stronger sense of a fully validated, exclusive derivation system (see the author’s related methodological distinction between these two claims).

Falsifiability and Future Plan

Falsifiability

The hypothesis makes concrete, checkable predictions: (i) newly deciphered dhātu, upasarga, and pratyaya should continue to resolve into coherent varṇa-samāsa readings without requiring new, ad hoc senses to be added to already-fixed varṇa entries; (ii) the corpus not yet examined — enumerated below — should show the same pattern; (iii) where a proposed reading cannot be made coherent without inflating a varṇa’s sense inventory, that is grounds to revise or reject the specific derivation, not to expand the varṇa’s meaning set post hoc. The varṇa dictionary itself was fixed as a time-stamped commitment in November 2025, prior to a number of the individual dhātu decipherments carried out afterward — a deliberate falsifiability control, since the building blocks were not free to be adjusted to fit each new word examined after that date.

The corpus not yet systematically examined includes: the remainder of the Aṣṭādhyāyī (all pāṭha — sūtra, gaṇa, uṇādi, liṅgānuśāsana, etc.) beyond what is reported here; and, beyond that, the Veda (वेद), Jyotiṣa (ज्योतिष), Kalpa (कल्प), Nirukta (निरुक्त), and Śikṣā (शिक्षा) literatures. The nipāta (निपात) subclass of avyaya (अव्यय) is explicitly flagged as unresolved: unlike upasarga, which this framework treats as already derived, nipāta derivation from varṇa meanings remains an open, falsifiable claim rather than an established result.

Future Plan

Two further programs of work follow directly from the findings above, and are laid out here in more detail.

Program 1: Archaeological and evolutionary fitment. Because textual and comparative-linguistic validation is foundationally limited by Saussurean arbitrariness once a comparison reaches beyond the depth at which conventional historical linguistics operates (roughly the last 5,000–10,000 years), independent validation for claims about deeper antiquity cannot come from text-internal or cross-language comparison alone. The intended validation path is instead fitment against evolutionary and archaeological evidence: (a) testing whether the depth-stratification internally proposed for varṇa (from proprioceptive/mechanoreceptive load-primitives, through maneuver/anchor-function primitives, to later object-and-action concepts) tracks independently established timelines in hominin cognitive and anatomical evolution; and (b) testing geographic and chronological claims — such as the proposed Gujarat/Cambay/Narmada proto-settlement geography inferred from directional Vedic vocabulary — against the archaeological record, with the caveat that sea-level rise may have submerged relevant fossil or material evidence if the originating population was shore-based.

Program 2: Neurocognitive grounding of varṇa meaning. The working hypothesis is that varṇa meanings are not socially assigned but are rooted in brain circuitry and cognition that predates conventional symbolic language — for example, the proposal that ṛ/ḷ encode dominance-hierarchy circuitry, and that the nasal-consonant class encodes proprioceptive load-type primitives (tension, compression, shear, torsion), places these particular varṇa at a deeper, pre-conceptual layer than the rest of the dictionary. This program proposes to test that placement directly against known findings in comparative neuroscience and developmental cognition, rather than against textual or archaeological evidence, and to identify which parts of the current dictionary are best understood as later, shallower elaborations of a smaller set of deep functional primitives.

Beyond these two programs, the varṇa-to-pada decipherment, if sustained across the wider corpus named above, is itself expected to yield a testable evolutionary history of language and, by extension, of the populations that spoke it — a research direction distinct from, but dependent on, Programs 1 and 2.

Conclusion

The assumption that a language sprang into being fully formed by a single, arbitrary act of assignment — its “Genesis” moment — is unreasonable on its face. Language, like other complex products of biological history, more plausibly evolved incrementally, and should be expected to show the marks of that history.

The non-random nature of Saṃskṛta proposed here, and the consequent claim that Saṃskṛta pada are, at bottom, fully derivable from varṇa meanings, opens two further fields of inquiry, developed above as Programs 1 and 2: (1) that the path from varṇa meaning to pada should, if traced carefully, recover an evolutionary history of language and, consequently, of humankind — the author’s preliminary findings suggest that language in some form predates Homo sapiens, and that its most primitive evolutionary layer predates the genus Homo entirely; and (2) that varṇa meanings themselves emerge from brain circuitry and cognition with roots that can, in principle, be identified and independently verified.
